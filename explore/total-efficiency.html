Efficiency starts before the power plant

This page is based on three papers: Petrakopoulou & García-Tenorio Corcuera (2022), “Introducing the total efficiency to address challenges of the 21st century”, Journal of Cleaner Production 374, 133409, Petrakopoulou & García-Tenorio (2023), “Evaluating hydrogen-based electricity generation using the concept of total efficiency”, Energy Conversion and Management 293, 117438, and García-Tenorio Corcuera & Petrakopoulou (2025), “Evaluating the impact of CO₂ capture and storage on total efficiency: A lifecycle analysis”, Cleaner Engineering and Technology 27, 101002.

Efficiency tells us how much of a fuel's energy a power plant turns into electricity. It is the number we use to compare power plants, set targets and write energy policy.

But efficiency only starts counting when the fuel arrives at the plant. Before that, gas and coal have to be extracted, cleaned, often liquefied, and carried for thousands of kilometres by pipeline, train and ship. Hydrogen first has to be made. All of this uses energy too, and most of it comes from more fossil fuel.

Total efficiency adds that journey to the calculation, together with what happens after the plant, such as capturing and storing CO₂, so that energy sources can be compared on fair terms.

Why a new measure?

A number that stops at the plant's fence

On paper, fossil-fuel power plants look far more efficient than renewable ones. Typical values reported in the literature:

Solar PV10–20%Fuel: sunlight, free and on site
Solar thermal≈30%Fuel: sunlight, free and on site
Wind turbine20–40%Fuel: wind, free and on site
Gas combined cycle>60%Fuel: gas, often from another continent

This comparison is not fair. Sun and wind are used where they arrive. Fossil fuels must be found, extracted, processed and shipped before a power plant can burn them, and conventional efficiency ignores every one of those steps. The same is true for hydrogen, which does not exist freely in nature and has to be produced first.

As Europe switches from coal to imported gas, and plans for hydrogen, these hidden steps are getting longer and more energy-hungry. A fair measure has to include them.

The idea

Count the journey, not just the burn

Total efficiency keeps the familiar definition of efficiency and adds one term to the bottom of the fraction: the energy spent to make the fuel ready for use.

Total efficiency= Electricity producedFuel burned in the plant + energy to extract, process and transport it
Direct energy use

Fuel burned along the way

Diesel in mining machines and trucks, heavy fuel oil in ships, gas burned to run a liquefaction plant. These are counted directly as extra fuel.

Indirect energy use

Electricity used along the way

Pumps, compressors, conveyor belts and electric trains use electricity. The method traces it back to the fuel burned to generate it, taking into account each country's power mix.

The energy data for each step come from published life cycle assessment studies. Because several studies report different values for the same step, the analysis runs many combinations and reports a range.

The next fuel

Hydrogen has to be made first

Hydrogen burns without CO₂, which makes it a candidate fuel for future power plants. But it has to be produced, compressed or liquefied, and transported. The second study applied total efficiency to hydrogen in two existing gas power plants, one in Spain and one in Germany, assuming they switch to hydrogen.

After the power plant

Capturing CO₂ costs energy too

Carbon capture and storage (CCS) removes most of the CO₂ from a power plant's exhaust and stores it underground. It is an important option for cutting emissions from gas and coal plants. But it is not free in energy terms: the chemical solvent that captures the CO₂ has to be heated to release it again, and the CO₂ then has to be compressed, piped away and injected.

The third study added these steps to total efficiency, for a gas plant and a coal plant in the UK and in Australia, with fuel from nearby fields and mines.

Why it matters

A fairer yardstick for the energy transition

Fair comparisons

Fossil, renewable and hydrogen-based electricity can be compared on the same basis, including everything it takes to bring the fuel to the plant.

Where energy is lost

Total efficiency shows which steps cost the most, such as long-distance shipping or liquefying gas, and where shorter supply chains or local production would help most.

Honest numbers for CO₂ capture

It shows the full energy price of capturing CO₂, which helps target improvements such as better solvents, heat recovery and shorter transport routes.

Better choices for hydrogen

It shows that hydrogen made from surplus renewable electricity close to where it is used keeps most of its value, while hydrogen made from imported gas or grid power loses most of it.
